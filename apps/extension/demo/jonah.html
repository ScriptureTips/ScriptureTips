When God doesn’t back you up: Jonah, and the only sign we get

Posted on 12 November 2026 · Filed under Old Testament, Prophets · 9 minute read

Most of us know the first two chapters of Jonah, and we love them for the wrong reasons: the storm, the great fish, the sailor’s prayer from the deep. The book is easy to retell as an adventure for children. But the part that has been following me around this month is the very end, which is a story about a man sitting outside a city, furious that nothing bad has happened to it.

He did what he was told

Start with the facts. God told Jonah to go to Nineveh and speak against it (Jonah 1:1–2). Jonah ran the other way, down to Joppa and onto a ship to Tarshish (Jon 1:3), as if the one who made the sea and the dry land could be left behind (compare Ps 139:7–10). Everyone knows what happened next: the storm, the lot, the fish, the three days, and the prayer that begins in despair and ends in “salvation belongs to the Lord” (Jonah 1:1–2:10).

What we skip is how thoroughly Jonah obeyed the second time. The word came to him again (Jonah 3:1–2), and this time he went. He walked a day into a vast city and said the one sentence he had been given (Jonah 3:4). He didn’t soften it, add a call to repent, or hint at mercy. He delivered the message exactly. And then, against every expectation, the people of Nineveh believed God, from the greatest to the least, and the king himself sat in ashes and ordered even the herds and flocks to fast (Jonah 3:5–8). When God saw what they did, he had mercy and changed his mind about the disaster (Jonah 3:9–10).

Here is the point where most sermons turn on Jonah and call him petty. I don’t think it’s that simple, and I think it’s worth giving him his due before we judge him.

Why he was angry

Read what the text says. It says that Jonah was greatly displeased and became angry (Jonah 3:10–4:1). Then he prays, and his prayer is the most honest sentence in the book: “I knew it.” He had fled to Tarshish because he knew that God is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abounding in love, a God who relents from sending calamity (Jonah 4:2). God asked him about that anger twice (Jonah 4:4, 9). Every phrase of that is lifted from the oldest creed in the Bible, God’s own self-description to Moses (Exodus 34:6–7), repeated after the golden calf (Num 14:18), and sung by the Psalmists (Psalm 86:15; Psalm 103:8) and the prophets (Joel 2:13).

So Jonah wasn’t ignorant about God. He was quoting scripture, correctly, and resenting it. That ought to unsettle every one of us who is good at quoting.

But notice also what was at stake for him. A prophet in Israel was tested by one thing: did his word come true (Deut 18:21–22)? Jonah had been vindicated like that before. He is the same Jonah son of Amittai who spoke for the Lord in the days of Jeroboam, and whose word then came to pass (2 Kgs 14:25). Now he had walked into the capital of the empire that would one day crush his people, announced its doom on God’s authority, and God didn’t follow through. He had done what he was told, and he was left standing there looking like a liar.

That is a real wound, and other prophets knew it. Elijah, after the greatest victory of his life, sat under a bush and asked to die (1 Kgs 19:4, 10). Jeremiah accused God of enticing him and then leaving him a laughing-stock all day long, and found that he couldn’t stop speaking anyway (Jer 20:7–9). Jonah’s own request is nearly the same, “it is better for me to die than to live” (Jonah 4:3). Resentment of this kind rarely comes from laziness. It comes from faithful people who feel that God didn’t hold up his end.

God’s answer is a question

Look at how God responds. He doesn’t argue the theology, and he doesn’t punish the outburst. He asks, “Is it right for you to be angry?” (Jonah 4:4), and when Jonah walks away to watch the city, God grows a plant to shade him, sends a worm to kill it, and asks the question again over the withered stalk (Jonah 4:6–9). The book closes on a question that has no answer written under it: should God not care about that great city, with more than a hundred and twenty thousand people who do not know their right hand from their left, and many animals as well (Jonah 4:10–11)?

Compare that ending to the one Jesus left us at the end of the lost son. The elder brother stands outside the party, angry that mercy has been given to the wrong person, and the father comes out to plead with him (Luke 15:25–32). We never learn whether he goes in. Neither do we learn whether Jonah does. Both stories stop at the door and hand the decision to the reader. The same note sounds in the workers who grumbled at the generous landowner (Matt 20:1–16): “Are you envious because I am generous?” (Matthew 20:15)

And the answer God gives is the same everywhere. He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but would have them turn and live (Ezek 33:11; see Ezekiel 18:23). He is patient with us, not wanting anyone to perish (2 Pet 3:9), and wants everyone to be saved (1 Tim 2:4). Mercy isn’t a loophole in his justice. It is, as James puts it, what triumphs over judgment (Jas 2:13). It isn’t fair by our arithmetic, which is exactly Paul’s point: “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy” (Rom 9:15–16, quoted from Exodus). We can’t have a God who is generous with us and stingy with Nineveh. His ways are higher than ours (Isaiah 55:8–9), and past finding out (Romans 11:33).

The sign of Jonah

This is why the way Jesus uses Jonah has stopped me in my tracks. Some of the scribes and Pharisees came to him and said, “Teacher, we want to see a sign from you” (Matt 12:38–42). They had seen him heal, cast out demons, and feed thousands. They weren’t short of evidence, and they asked for a sign anyway, which is what a hardened heart does. Jesus called them a wicked and adulterous generation and told them that no sign would be given to them but the sign of the prophet Jonah. The same exchange is recorded in Luke 11:29–32, and again, when the Pharisees test him in the district of Dalmanutha (Matt 16:1–4; Mark 8:11–12), where Mark says he sighed deeply in his spirit.

What was the sign? Jesus gives it himself. “For as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth” (Matthew 12:40). The sign is a death that is not the end. It is the thing Paul says is of first importance, that Christ died for our sins and was raised on the third day (1 Cor 15:3–4). When they asked for a sign at the temple, he said the same in a riddle: destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up (John 2:18–22). Even his enemies remembered it. They went to Pilate after the crucifixion and said that “that deceiver” had claimed he would rise after three days (Matt 27:62–64).

But Jesus adds something that gets less attention. The men of Nineveh will stand up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, because they repented at the preaching of Jonah, “and behold, something greater than Jonah is here” (Luke 11:32). Think about what he is saying. Jonah was a reluctant, resentful messenger who gave one sentence and hoped it would fail, and a pagan city repented. These people had Jesus himself, and they wanted a sign (Matt 12:41).

The contrast between the two preachers is the whole argument. Jonah sat east of the city, waiting to see whether it would burn. Jesus wept over the city that would kill him (Luke 19:41–44), and from the cross prayed for the men who drove in the nails (Luke 23:34). When two of his disciples wanted to call down fire on a village that wouldn’t receive him, he turned and rebuked them (Luke 9:51–56). Nineveh, for all that, did not stay repentant for ever, and Nahum’s oracle shows the patience of God has a longer fuse than ours, not no fuse at all (Nah 3:1). But the shape of the story is the same: the God who relents is looking for people who will.

What I take from it

I can’t read Jonah 4 without recognizing myself. I have prayed for people to get what they deserved, and I have felt cheated when they got mercy instead. I have also done what I believed God asked and felt that he left me exposed. Jonah gives me permission to say that out loud, because he said it, and God answered him gently. But he doesn’t let me stay there. The sign for a generation that wants proof is a Savior who went into the earth for three days on behalf of the people we would have written off, and the only fitting reply is to love our enemies and pray for those who persecute us, so that we may be children of our Father in heaven (Matt 5:43–48).

If you only have time for one passage this week, read Jonah 4 slowly, twice, and then Luke 15:25–32. Or if you prefer the short version, here is the whole gospel in one verse, John 3:16 (KJV), which I now read with Nineveh in mind.

Should I not have concern for that great city? — Jonah 4:11

A note for the readers who always ask: yes, I know the “three days and three nights” debate. The short answer is that in the idiom of the time any part of a day counted as a day, and Matthew 12:40 sits inside a conversation that Mark 8:31 reports as “after three days.” I’ll give it a post of its own. In the meantime the commentary I rely on has a good discussion on Matthew 12:40, linked here by its author, so I haven’t touched it. For the original language, the Hebrew word in Jonah 4:2 for “relents” is the same one used in Exod 32:14, and it is worth ten minutes with a lexicon (Romans 9:15 (BSB) makes the same point from the other direction).
